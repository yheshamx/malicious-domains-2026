The domain abuse business is a registration business

A five month scan of 1.52 million freshly flagged domains finds that almost nine in ten were registered by attackers rather than stolen, bought in same day bulk batches, and burned within weeks. Here is what it measured, what it recommends, and where it does not hold up.

Mashood, F. and Nabeel, M. “A Longitudinal Study of Recently Observed Malicious Domains: Characteristics, Infrastructure, and Abuse Patterns.” Southern New Hampshire University, National University. 9 June 2026. CC BY 4.0.

What was measured

1.52 million domains, five months, three data sources joined

Every second level domain that VirusTotal first saw between January and May 2026 and that at least five independent scanning engines flagged as malicious. That set was joined to WHOIS registration records, Farsight passive DNS resolution history, and the Tranco top 1M popularity list.

Before computing anything, the authors split the set in two, because hijacked legitimate sites and purpose built attack domains behave nothing alike. A domain counts as compromised if it ranks in the Tranco top 500K, or if it was registered at least three years before detection. Everything else counts as attacker created.

The compromised class is almost entirely the age rule, which means old domains getting repurposed is the compromise story, not front page sites getting hijacked. The authors note 89.3% is a conservative floor, since any attacker domain aged past three years before use gets counted on the other side.

Coordination

Bulk registration is not a tactic anymore, it is the default

The authors group attack domains by (registrar, WHOIS creation date) and call any group of five or more a batch. On that definition, 1,057,926 of 1,357,921 attack domains (77.9%) belong to a batch, across 22,977 distinct batches. Average batch size works out to about 46 domains†. Names inside batches follow programmatic patterns like 10jfr.top and 10jgr.top.

At the IP level it is even starker. Eight of the top ten hosting addresses are Cloudflare, spread across the 188.114.0.0/16, 172.64.0.0/16 and 2a06:98c1::/32 ranges. The two busiest IPv4 addresses front 231,685 and 231,618 distinct attack domains each. That is reverse proxying used as origin masking at industrial scale.

AS398823 hosts 523,395 attack domains, second only to Cloudflare, and the paper never identifies the operator. That is the single most actionable unknown in the study, and it is left blank.

A quarter of a million to a third of a million newly flagged domains every month, with an unexplained January outlier the paper never accounts for. Read the flat band as the real signal, not the spike, and do not read February through May as a decline.

What the authors recommend

Seven named interventions

  1. Rate limit and review same day bulk registrations at the registrar. Their top intervention, because 78% of abuse fits that one shape.
  2. Engage the ten largest registrars directly with stronger anti abuse policy and faster takedown, since they carry 59% of the volume between them.
  3. Triage by passive DNS query volume, not by domain count. Sinkhole the high volume tail first and you cut end user exposure far faster.
  4. Get DNS providers flagging domains that resolve into known malicious IP footprints, using the hub and spoke hosting structure as the detector.
  5. Wire automated takedown pipelines straight into Cloudflare and AWS abuse channels. Both publish reporting mechanisms; the scale of misuse says manual reporting cannot keep pace.
  6. Run automated brand monitoring for high value targets, matching combosquat and typosquat variants continuously rather than on complaint.
  7. Split compromised from attacker created before computing any statistic. Skip this and hijacked legitimate sites skew every distribution you produce.
Read this before you quote it

Six places the study does not hold up

  1. There is no benign base rate anywhere in the paper. Every ranking is a raw count, never abuse per thousand registered. .com leads because .com is the biggest TLD. Cloudflare leads because Cloudflare fronts a huge share of the whole web. You cannot separate an abusive registrar from a merely large one with this data, and the registrar engagement recommendation depends entirely on that distinction.
  2. Registrar names were never normalised. Dynadot and Namecheap each appear twice under different spellings, which reorders the leaderboard once merged.
  3. Denominators switch mid section. The top four registrars at 36% is computed against all 1,357,921 attack domains, while the top ten at 59.2% is computed against only those with a known registrar (about 1,204,760†). On a single denominator the top four is 40.7%†.
  4. Brand matching is noisy and presented as clean. Substring hits on generic words are ranked alongside real impersonation with no caveat.
  5. The compromised split rests on two crude heuristics. Tranco rank or three years of age, nothing else. To their credit the authors say so and the error direction is safe, so 89.3% stands as a floor.
  6. The second largest hosting ASN is never identified. 523,395 attack domains and no operator name attached.
† marks a figure derived here from the paper's published counts, not printed in the paper itself. Every other number is quoted directly.
